After a match most of us are left with a feeling: "I missed a lot", "my partner was unplayable", "we broke them early". The trouble is that the feeling tends to remember the three most striking points and forget the other eighty. Recording the match point by point swaps that conversation for one with numbers.

These are the statistics worth looking at, what each one means and what to do with them.

## The three ways a point ends

Every padel point ends in one of three ways, and each is credited to a player.

### Winner

A winning shot: the ball is in and the opponent cannot return it. A smash that leaves the court, a volley at the feet, a bandeja that dies in the fence. It is credited to the player who hit it.

### Unforced error

Your own mistake on a ball you could have played without pressure: an easy volley into the net, a double fault, a lob that sails long with nobody pushing you. It is credited to the player who missed.

### Forced error generated

The opponent misses, but because your shot made them: a ball that was very fast, very wide or very low that they barely touched. Their error ended the point, but the credit is yours, so it goes to the player who **generated** the error.

This third category is the one most often lost when you count from memory. A player who hits no spectacular winners but keeps the opponents uncomfortable may be winning more points than their partner, and without recording it nobody sees it.

## Effectiveness: one number per player

Those three counts give you the most useful indicator:

**Effectiveness = (winners + forced errors generated) ÷ (winners + forced errors generated + unforced errors)**

It is the share of the points you decided that went your way.

An example: in a match you hit 6 winners, generated 5 forced errors and made 9 unforced errors. You decided 20 points, and 11 went your way: your effectiveness is 55%.

Two notes for reading it properly:

- **It does not measure how involved you were.** A player with few actions can have a high effectiveness. Always read it alongside the number of points they decided.
- **Compare against yourself.** The number depends on the level of the opponents and on your role in the pair. What helps is seeing how your own percentage moves from one match to the next.

## What the team numbers show

### Points won

The pair's total. It sounds obvious, but it is common to win a match having won fewer points than the opponents, because not all points weigh the same. When that happens, it is worth checking which games the points were lost in.

### Breaks

A break is a game won while the other pair was serving. In padel the serve gives less of an edge than in tennis, so breaks are more frequent, but they are still the games that move the score. If you keep losing your serve, the problem is usually in the first two shots: the serve and the first volley.

### Streaks

The longest run of consecutive points each pair won. A long streak against you usually matches a moment you can pinpoint: a change of ends, a long game lost, a tactical switch by the opponents.

### Momentum

A chart that climbs when one pair wins points and drops when the other does. It shows at a glance what the final score hides: whether the match was even from start to finish or was decided in a ten-minute stretch.

## How to use all this to train

1. **Record at least three matches before drawing conclusions.** A single match depends too much on the opponents and the day.
2. **Start with unforced errors.** It is the statistic that depends only on you, and reducing it usually pays off faster than adding winners.
3. **Check who decides the points in your pair.** If one of you accounts for nearly all the unforced errors, it may be because every ball is being played to them. That is a tactical issue, not a technical one.
4. **Pick one thing to work on.** For example, taking effectiveness from 50% to 60%, or losing one fewer service game per set. Measure again a few weeks later.
5. **Have someone record from outside if you can.** Playing and recording at once is hard. Ideally someone watching keeps the record, or you do it between points.
